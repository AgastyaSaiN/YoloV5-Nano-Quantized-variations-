INT8 Quantization of YOLOv5n for an Embedded NPU Target

Benchmark report  |  YOLOv5n v7.0 (original, anchor-based)  |  COCO val2017, all 5000 images  |  ONNX Runtime, TFLite and OpenVINO, CPU, 4 pinned cores

Summary. We compared an FP32 YOLOv5n baseline (mAP50-95 0.281, matching the published figure) with three INT8 designs, each quantized per-channel and per-tensor, in both ONNX and TFLite. The designs differ in which parts of the network stay in FP32: M1 none, M2 neck and head, M3 head only.

  • Best accuracy: M2 per-channel keeps 95.8% of FP32 accuracy in ONNX (95.2% in TFLite) at 1.7x compression.
  • Best trade-off: M3 per-channel keeps 94.9% (ONNX) and 94.0% (TFLite) at 2.9x and 3.0x compression (2.78 MB and 2.67 MB).
  • Per-channel beats per-tensor in every pairing, by 0.8 to 1.3 mAP points in the earlier (640 px) measurement, where the difference is statistically real in all of them.
  • The detection head is the sensitive part. Quantizing the head alone costs 7.0% accuracy; every other single layer costs 1.0% or less. Keeping the head in FP32 is worth 1.8 to 1.9 mAP points.
  • A fully INT8 model works once box coordinates are normalised to 0-1 (89.7% retained in ONNX, 87.8% in TFLite). With pixel-scale boxes it scores zero.
  • Speed and memory on this CPU: INT8 ONNX models take 38 to 48% less time per image than FP32 and all-INT8 TFLite models 63% less; TFLite pipelines that keep an FP32 stage 50 to 60% less (all against the 640 px FP32 baseline; the INT8 models run at 512 px). The all-INT8 TFLite pipelines also use about 53% less memory. These are desktop-CPU figures and do not transfer to an NPU (Section 7).
  • Input size: the INT8 models take a 512 x 512 picture instead of 640 x 640, which removes about a third of the computation. At 512 px the OpenVINO M3 model is 2.18x faster than the 640 px FP32 model at 95.7% of its accuracy. See the sections after the six benchmarks.

Reading the charts

How to read it. The FP32 model scores 0.461 (mAP@0.5). M2 (neck and head kept FP32) is the most accurate INT8 design in every runtime (0.442, 0.438 and 0.444), M3 is next, and the all-INT8 M1 is the least accurate (as low as 0.429), because every layer that stays FP32 cannot lose accuracy to rounding. The INT8 models also look at a smaller 512 px picture than the 640 px FP32 model, which costs a little more, since there are fewer pixels to find small objects with. The three runtimes use different quantizers, so the same design scores slightly differently in each.

How to read it. The FP32 model scores 0.2811 (mAP@0.5:0.95). M2 (neck and head kept FP32) is the most accurate INT8 design in every runtime (0.2694, 0.2677 and 0.2713), M3 is next, and the all-INT8 M1 is the least accurate (as low as 0.2469), because every layer that stays FP32 cannot lose accuracy to rounding. The INT8 models also look at a smaller 512 px picture than the 640 px FP32 model, which costs a little more, since there are fewer pixels to find small objects with. The three runtimes use different quantizers, so the same design scores slightly differently in each.

What the sizes mean. The size is the sum of the files a runtime needs: one file for ONNX Runtime, one .tflite file for TFLite, and the .xml graph plus the .bin weights for OpenVINO. M1 is the smallest in every runtime (2.28 MB ONNX Runtime, 2.15 MB TFLite, 3.13 MB OpenVINO) because every weight is stored as 8 bits. M3 is a little larger because its detection head stays FP32 (2.78, 2.67 and 3.46 MB), and M2 is the largest INT8 design because the neck and the head both stay FP32 (4.80, 4.73 and 5.39 MB). The FP32 model is 7.92 MB. The input size does not change the file size, because the weights are the same at 512 px and 640 px. The OpenVINO files are about 1 MB larger than the others, which matches the separate .xml graph file of about 1 MB.

Why the order is not the same in every runtime. Quantizing more of the network does not always make it faster. In ONNX Runtime, M2 (14.4 ms) is faster than the all-INT8 M1 (17.1 ms): in this runtime the neck's resize and concatenate steps need extra conversion work when they are INT8, which costs more than the INT8 convolutions save (a per-operation profile of the network showed this). TFLite and OpenVINO behave the other way round: M1 is fastest (10.2 ms and 10.0 ms) and M2 slowest (13.8 and 12.1 ms), consistent with the FP32 neck of M2 being the expensive part there. The FP32 models take 27.5 ms (ONNX Runtime), 27.6 ms (TFLite) and 23.0 ms (OpenVINO), so every INT8 model is faster than its FP32 reference; part of the gain comes from the smaller 512 px input.

How to read the memory. This is the extra memory the process needs for the model while it runs, above a process that has only loaded the runtime. It includes the weights, the intermediate tensors (activations) and the runtime's working buffers. For ONNX Runtime and TFLite the all-INT8 model uses the least (34 and 47 MB), against 61 and 99 MB for FP32, because 8-bit tensors take a quarter of the space; M2 and M3 use more because their FP32 parts keep 32-bit tensors. OpenVINO is different: its INT8 models use more memory than its FP32 model (104 MB for M1 against 89 MB for FP32), and M1 is not the lowest. This is the measured value. A likely cause is the memory OpenVINO uses to read and compile a quantized graph, which contains many extra conversion nodes, but this was not verified. Peak process memory includes that compile step, so it is the right number for a device that must load the model, and the wrong one for judging the steady-state size of the activations.

How to read F1. F1 combines precision (how many reported objects are right) and recall (how many real objects are found) at confidence threshold 0.25. FP32 scores 0.552; the quantized models lose recall and keep most of their precision, so F1 falls by 2.1 points for ONNX Runtime M2 and 3.1 for M1. The designs rank as in mAP.

1. Setup

Stage shown. Tables 2 to 5 and Figures 1, 4, 5 and 6 show the models: the INT8 models built at 512 x 512 input, with the 640 x 640 FP32 model as the baseline they replace. The studies in Sections 3, 4, 8 and 9 (box scale, layer sensitivity, calibration size, statistics) were done on the earlier 640 x 640 models and are labelled as such.

Models. YOLOv5n has a backbone (layers 0-9), a PANet neck (10-23) and a Detect head (24). Table 1 lists the designs. Each INT8 design is built twice: per-channel (one scale per output channel of each convolution) and per-tensor (one scale per layer).

Evaluation. All 5000 COCO val2017 images, scored with the official pycocotools, with preprocessing and post-processing (square letterbox at the model's input size (640 or 512), confidence 0.001, NMS IoU 0.6, up to 300 detections) matching the official YOLOv5 validation. The FP32 model scores 0.281, in line with the published 28.0.

Statistics. To tell real differences from noise, the 5000 images are split into 10 disjoint folds; each model is scored per fold and paired differences get a 95% t-interval (Table 6).

Per-channel versus per-tensor. In all six pairings per-channel is better, by 0.8 to 1.3 mAP points (Table 6). Convolution output channels in this network have quite different weight ranges, and one shared scale wastes resolution on the narrow ones.

How much to quantize. Moving from M2 to M3 (also quantizing the neck) costs a further 0.3 mAP points in ONNX per-channel, while shrinking the file from 4.80 to 2.78 MB. That is a good price for the saving.

ONNX versus TFLite. ONNX is 0.1 to 0.5 mAP points better than TFLite for the same design (earlier 640 px models: 0.4 to 0.9). To test the calibration explanation directly (earlier 640 px models), the ONNX M3 per-channel model was rebuilt with min-max calibration (the method the TFLite converter uses): it scores 0.2676, against 0.2735 with percentile calibration and 0.2677 for the TFLite model. Min-max calibration therefore reproduces the TFLite accuracy (difference 0.00 mAP points), so the calibration method accounts for essentially all of the ONNX-to-TFLite gap for this design.

Object size. Small objects have the lowest AP in every model. When the head is also INT8 (M1) they are hit hardest: 20 to 30% of small-object AP is lost, against 5 to 11% for large objects. With an FP32 head (M2, M3) the loss is spread across sizes, and no size class is consistently worst.

3. The box-scale finding

The detector outputs one table holding box coordinates and confidence scores. With boxes up to 640 and scores below 1 sharing one INT8 scale, each INT8 step is about 2.5 wide, so every score rounds to zero and nothing is detected. Dividing the box output by 640 (done by changing the decode constants, with no added operations, and exactly reversible: the normalised FP32 model reproduces the original to within 6e-5 pixels) puts both on a comparable scale. M1 then recovers from 0.000 to 0.255 (ONNX per-channel, 640 px). The official YOLOv5 TFLite export does the same, which is why the TFLite models use it throughout.

Even so, M1 remains below M3 by 1.8 to 1.9 mAP points, so the head is still worth protecting if the deployment allows an FP32 stage.

4. Layer sensitivity

The Detect head (layer 24) costs 7.0%, far more than any other layer that was actually quantized. The next largest are layer 23, the last neck block feeding the head (1.0%), and layer 2 (0.9%). Caveat: in this test, layers 11 and 15 (the upsample layers, which hold no weights) received no quantization at all when isolated, because ONNX Runtime inserts no quantize/dequantize operations around them on their own. Their zero is therefore not a result, and they are drawn hatched in Figure 3. All other layers were confirmed to contain quantization operations. The study supports protecting the output end of the network; it does not show that any particular layer is free to quantize.

Speed. Quantization is working: ONNX Runtime executes the INT8 designs with integer convolution kernels. Each model was timed over 9 interleaved rounds (fresh process per round). On this CPU, INT8 ONNX models take 38 to 48% less time per image than FP32, the all-INT8 TFLite models 63% less, and the TFLite designs that keep an FP32 stage (M2, M3) 50 to 60% less, because those layers still run as float. Each ONNX INT8 model was faster than the FP32 model in 9 of the 9 rounds, and each TFLite INT8 pipeline in 9 of 9. Timing is noisy, though: the same model varied by up to 10% between rounds (laptop CPU frequency and temperature drift), so small gaps between INT8 variants (for example per-channel versus per-tensor) are not meaningful, while the gap to FP32 is. A per-operation profile of the earlier 640 px ONNX models explains why the gain is not larger: INT8 convolutions are faster (8.0 ms against 12.6 ms for FP32), but the SiLU activation (a sigmoid followed by a multiply) costs 5.3 ms in the INT8 model against 0.5 ms in FP32, where it is fused into the convolution, so most of the convolution saving is spent on activations. Quantizing the neck does not speed it up: the neck region takes 11.1 ms in M3 and 10.5 ms in M1 against 5.3 ms in FP32, which is why M2 (neck left in FP32) is the fastest ONNX design. (Profiler kernel times include profiling overhead and do not add up to wall-clock time; this is how ONNX Runtime on this CPU behaves and says nothing about an NPU.) None of this is NPU speed. An NPU executes only INT8 operations, so on a real board the FP32 stages of M2 and M3 would fall back to the host CPU, and the ranking could differ substantially. That needs measuring on the target hardware.

Memory. Table 5 gives the memory a model adds to a process that already has the runtime loaded, at load time and at its peak during inference (which includes the activation tensors). The FP32 ONNX model adds 61 MB at peak; the all-INT8 ONNX models add 44% less. The ONNX models that keep FP32 parts (M2, M3) add 16 to 18% less: they save less than the all-INT8 models because the FP32 parts keep full-size tensors (a likely explanation, not verified). In TFLite the effect is clearer: the FP32 pipeline adds 99 MB, the all-INT8 pipelines 53% less, and M2 and M3 40 to 46% less. Memory added at load is similar across ONNX models (about 16 to 17 MB), probably because the runtime re-packs weights whatever their precision (not verified). The runtime's own baseline (about 56 MB for ONNX Runtime and 61 MB for TensorFlow) is excluded. On a memory-limited board, activation memory matters more than the file size.

Why ONNX and TFLite differ for the same design

The FP32 baselines are identical (mAP50-95 0.2811). Quantization is not: ONNX Runtime calibrates with percentile clipping, while the TFLite converter only offers min-max. Percentile ignores rare outliers and so gives finer steps for the typical values. Rebuilding the ONNX models with min-max calibration removes most of the gap (table below, measured on 640 px models): the remaining differences are in the range of -0.18 to +0.10 mAP points, while the percentile versions are +0.37 to +0.77 points above TFLite. The gap is a calibration effect, not a property of the format.

Why INT8 is not 2x or 4x faster here

  • The convolutions are only part of the time. In FP32 the SiLU activation is fused into the convolution. In INT8 it is a separate sigmoid and multiply on every layer, and its cost grows with the size of the activation tensor, which is largest in the first layers.
  • Other operators do not speed up: in ONNX Runtime the Resize and Concat steps of the neck need extra requantization work, so quantizing the neck (M3) is slower than leaving it in FP32 (M2). This was seen in the per-operation profile (results/profile.json).
  • Result with the smaller input size: the INT8 models take a 512 px picture instead of 640 px, which removes about a third of the computation, and run 2.18x (OpenVINO M3), 2.72x (TFLite M1) and 1.90x (ONNX Runtime M2) faster than the 640 px FP32 model of the same runtime, at 94 to 96% of its accuracy.
  • A 4x gain needs hardware where INT8 is not just faster but the only path (an NPU); this CPU cannot show that.

The input size

Computation falls with the square of the input size, so the INT8 models take a 512 x 512 picture while the FP32 reference takes 640 x 640 (512 x 512 is 0.64 of 640 x 640). The same network at 512 px without quantization scores 97.0% of the 640 px FP32 accuracy, so the smaller picture itself costs about 3 points and quantization costs the rest. Small objects are the ones lost first, so a smaller input suits applications whose objects are not tiny.

TFLite: how the models are made and run

The TFLite models follow the standard TensorFlow Lite / LiteRT recipe for post-training integer quantization (see the sources below):

  • Representative data. A set of typical inputs, preprocessed exactly like at inference, gives the converter the range of every activation. Here: 500 train2017 pictures letterboxed to the model's input size (the LiteRT example uses 100).
  • Weights and activations. Weights are int8, symmetric, per output channel in convolutions by default (the “channel-wise” models) or one scale per tensor (the “layer-wise” models, a converter switch); activations are int8, one scale per tensor. TFLite's converter offers only min-max range calibration, which is why it differs from the percentile calibration used for ONNX Runtime.
  • Keeping layers in float inside one file. M2 and M3 keep part of the network in float. The converter's quantization debugger can exclude chosen operations (denylisted_nodes), so the whole model stays in one .tflite file. The converted graph has lost the layer names, so the excluded operations are found from the structure: everything that is not an ancestor of the backbone outputs (M2) or of the neck outputs (M3); the three boundary tensors are located by comparing values with separately converted parts. In M3 the 57 backbone and neck convolutions are INT8 and the 3 Detect-head convolutions are float. The documentation notes that on integer-only accelerators float layers fragment the model, which is why the all-INT8 M1 is the design to try first on an NPU.
  • Integer input and output. Integer-only targets usually also need int8 model inputs and outputs. The rows marked “integer input/output” are M1 built this way (the program that runs them quantizes the picture and dequantizes the result with the model's own scales).
  • Check the FP32 conversion first. The FP32 TFLite model scores the same as the FP32 ONNX model (0.2811), so conversion itself loses nothing and all differences come from quantization.

The runtime mattered more than the model. The TFLite models looked slow next to ONNX Runtime and OpenVINO, but the same files run much faster in the current LiteRT interpreter than in the one built into TensorFlow 2.15 (table below). INT8 TFLite models are now among the fastest in this report, and they use less memory too. All TFLite timings and memory figures in this report were measured with LiteRT 2.3.0; accuracy is unaffected by the choice of runtime (checked on 500 images for M3 per-channel: the same score with both). The gain is specific to INT8: the float model runs about the same in both interpreters.

10. Recommendations

  • Use per-channel quantization wherever the target runtime supports it.
  • Normalise the box output to 0-1 (divide by the input size) before quantizing any model that includes the head.
  • For accuracy first, choose M2; for the best size-accuracy balance, choose M3; choose M1 only if the whole model must run as INT8 (for example entirely on an NPU), accepting the accuracy cost.
  • If speed matters more than the last few points of accuracy, lower the input size: at 512 px the M2 and M3 models keep 93 to 97% of the FP32 accuracy in all three runtimes and run 1.7 to 2.2 times as fast as the 640 px FP32 model of the same runtime. Check that the objects of interest are not tiny.
  • Use a compiled non-maximum-suppression routine; the reference numpy one took longer than the model.
  • Do not spend effort on calibration set size beyond about 100 images for this model (earlier 640 px study).
  • Next: time the candidates on the target board; try channel pruning and quantization-aware training; try a mixed design that keeps only the most sensitive layers (23 and 24) in FP32.

11. Limitations

  • No NPU measurements. Timing is from a laptop CPU (ONNX Runtime, TFLite with XNNPACK and OpenVINO). It is a proxy only, and the ranking of designs differs between runtimes (see the notes under the charts); the exact target board is not known.
  • Different calibration methods in ONNX (percentile) and TFLite (min-max) mean the two formats are not like-for-like; the TFLite converter offers no percentile option. OpenVINO models use a third quantizer (NNCF), so ONNX Runtime and OpenVINO are different quantizers and runtimes, not one model on two engines.
  • Memory is the increase in resident memory of a desktop process (sampled every 2 ms), not NPU or device memory, and it excludes the runtime's own baseline. OpenVINO's INT8 models use more than its FP32 model; the reason (probably compiling the quantized graph) was not verified.
  • Timing noise: round-to-round spread is a few percent for most models and occasionally much larger; the median of nine interleaved rounds is used. Differences of a few percent between INT8 variants are not meaningful.
  • Layer sensitivity uses the first 1000 images and one layer at a time; interactions between layers are not captured. The sensitivity, calibration-size, box-scale and statistical studies were run on the earlier 640 px models.
  • Data. COCO val2017 only. Results on other image domains may differ.

Appendix. Terms

mAP50-95 / mAP50
The standard detector accuracy score (0 to 1), averaged over overlap thresholds 0.50 to 0.95, or at 0.50 only.
Precision, recall, F1
Of reported detections, the share that are right; of real objects, the share found; and their harmonic mean.
Per-channel / per-tensor
One INT8 scale per output channel of each convolution, or one per layer.
Calibration
Running sample images through the model to choose the INT8 value ranges. Percentile ignores rare outliers; min-max uses the extremes.
Backbone, neck (PANet), head
The three stages of YOLOv5: feature extraction, feature fusion, and the Detect layer that outputs boxes, objects and classes.
Compression ratio
FP32 file size divided by the model's file size (same format).
