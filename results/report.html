INT8 Quantization of YOLOv5n for an Embedded NPU Target

Day 2 benchmark report  |  YOLOv5n v7.0 (original, anchor-based)  |  COCO val2017, all 5000 images  |  ONNX Runtime and TFLite, CPU, 4 threads

Summary. We compared an FP32 YOLOv5n baseline (mAP50-95 0.281, matching the published figure) with three INT8 designs, each quantized per-channel and per-tensor, in both ONNX and TFLite. The designs differ in which parts of the network stay in FP32: M1 none, M2 neck and head, M3 head only.

  • Best accuracy: M2 per-channel keeps 98.4% of FP32 accuracy in ONNX (97.0% in TFLite) at 1.6x compression.
  • Best trade-off: M3 per-channel keeps 97.3% (ONNX) and 95.2% (TFLite) at 2.7x and 2.8x compression (2.92 MB and 2.81 MB).
  • Per-channel beats per-tensor in every pairing, by 0.8 to 1.3 mAP points, and the difference is statistically real in all of them.
  • The detection head is the sensitive part. Quantizing the head alone costs 7.0% accuracy; every other single layer costs 1.0% or less. Keeping the head in FP32 is worth 1.8 to 1.9 mAP points.
  • A fully INT8 model works once box coordinates are normalised to 0-1 (90.8% retained in ONNX, 88.6% in TFLite). With pixel-scale boxes it scores zero.
  • Speed and memory on this CPU: INT8 ONNX models take 11 to 25% less time per image than FP32 and all-INT8 TFLite models 21 to 22% less; TFLite pipelines that keep an FP32 stage gain only 4 to 11%. The all-INT8 TFLite pipelines also use about 35 to 36% less memory. These are desktop-CPU figures and do not transfer to an NPU (Section 7).
  • Speed with ReLU activations (R55): replacing the 12 largest SiLU activations with ReLU, fine-tuned with the Detect head frozen and bit-identical, gives 1.7 to 1.8x in OpenVINO at 92 to 93% of the FP32 accuracy (about 2.1x over the ONNX Runtime FP32 baseline). SiLU-only INT8 reaches 1.3 to 1.5x at 97 to 98%. Neither reaches 2x at 95% or more; see “Why INT8 is not 2x or 4x faster here” below.

Implementations by period

The work is grouped into three periods. Each period is described below; the charts and tables then show the same benchmarks for all three, older periods in lighter shades. The Detect head is untouched in every model of every period.

Day 2 implementations

  • FP32 YOLOv5n baseline and three INT8 designs: M1 everything INT8, M2 backbone INT8 with neck and head FP32, M3 backbone and neck INT8 with the head FP32.
  • Each design in two granularities (per-channel and per-tensor) and two formats: ONNX (ONNX Runtime, percentile calibration) and TFLite (full-integer, min-max calibration, split into separate files so part of the network can stay FP32).
  • Box coordinates normalised to 0-1 so that the all-INT8 model works. Calibration on 500 train2017 images; testing on all 5000 val2017 images with the official COCO evaluation.
  • Extra studies: layer sensitivity, calibration-set size, 10-fold statistical comparison.

Day 3 implementations

  • Audit and corrections: pinned cores for timing, an idle machine, matched calibration methods for the ONNX versus TFLite comparison, and official COCO matching for precision and recall.
  • OpenVINO quantization (NNCF) of the same designs: the smallest files and a faster INT8 runtime on this CPU.
  • ReLU-activation models: R55 (12 of 57 activations replaced, 55% of the activation work) and R80 (29 of 57, 81%), fine-tuned with the Detect head frozen. They are faster but less accurate (see the tables and the speed-versus-accuracy chart).

Current stage

  • Reduced input size: the original network exported at 512, 448 and 416 pixels and quantized with the same OpenVINO procedure, no retraining. At 512 pixels M3 is 2.14x faster than the 640 px FP32 model at 95.7% of its accuracy; at 448 pixels 2.73x at 92.2%.
  • Compiled post-processing with identical output (12.5 ms down to 3.1 ms per image at confidence 0.25).
  • Tried and not adopted: fine-tuning at the smaller size, with or without ReLU, scored below the un-retrained network at the same size.
  • Not yet tried: channel pruning and quantization-aware training.

1. Setup

Models. YOLOv5n has a backbone (layers 0-9), a PANet neck (10-23) and a Detect head (24). Table 1 lists the designs. Each INT8 design is built twice: per-channel (one scale per output channel of each convolution) and per-tensor (one scale per layer).

Table 1. The designs compared.
IDBackboneNeck (PANet)Head (Detect)Variants
M0FP32FP32FP32baseline
M1INT8INT8INT8per-channel, per-tensor
M2INT8FP32FP32per-channel, per-tensor
M3INT8INT8FP32per-channel, per-tensor

Quantization. Static post-training INT8: weights signed 8-bit, activations 8-bit. In ONNX, ONNX Runtime QDQ with percentile calibration, keeping FP32 layers out by node name. In TFLite the converter cannot easily keep part of a network in FP32, so the network is split at the backbone/neck/head boundaries into separate files; INT8 stages use full-integer conversion with min-max calibration, FP32 stages stay float, and a runner chains them. All variants calibrate on the same 500 images from COCO train2017.

Evaluation. All 5000 COCO val2017 images, scored with the official pycocotools, with preprocessing and post-processing (square 640 letterbox, confidence 0.001, NMS IoU 0.6, up to 300 detections) matching the official YOLOv5 validation. The FP32 model scores 0.281, in line with the published 28.0.

Statistics. To tell real differences from noise, the 5000 images are split into 10 disjoint folds; each model is scored per fold and paired differences get a 95% t-interval (Table 6).

Per-channel versus per-tensor. In all six pairings per-channel is better, by 0.8 to 1.3 mAP points (Table 6). Convolution output channels in this network have quite different weight ranges, and one shared scale wastes resolution on the narrow ones.

How much to quantize. Moving from M2 to M3 (also quantizing the neck) costs a further 0.3 mAP points in ONNX per-channel, while shrinking the file from 4.94 to 2.92 MB. That is a good price for the saving.

ONNX versus TFLite. ONNX is 0.4 to 0.9 mAP points better than TFLite for the same design. To test the calibration explanation directly, the ONNX M3 per-channel model was rebuilt with min-max calibration (the method the TFLite converter uses): it scores 0.2676, against 0.2735 with percentile calibration and 0.2677 for the TFLite model. Min-max calibration therefore reproduces the TFLite accuracy (difference 0.00 mAP points), so the calibration method accounts for essentially all of the ONNX-to-TFLite gap for this design.

Object size. Small objects have the lowest AP in every model. When the head is also INT8 (M1) they are hit hardest: 20 to 30% of small-object AP is lost, against 5 to 11% for large objects. With an FP32 head (M2, M3) the loss is spread across sizes, and no size class is consistently worst.

3. The box-scale finding

The detector outputs one table holding box coordinates and confidence scores. With boxes up to 640 and scores below 1 sharing one INT8 scale, each INT8 step is about 2.5 wide, so every score rounds to zero and nothing is detected. Dividing the box output by 640 (done by changing the decode constants, with no added operations, and exactly reversible: the normalised FP32 model reproduces the original to within 6e-5 pixels) puts both on a comparable scale. M1 then recovers from 0.000 to 0.255 (ONNX per-channel). The official YOLOv5 TFLite export does the same, which is why the TFLite models use it throughout.

Even so, M1 remains below M3 by 1.8 to 1.9 mAP points, so the head is still worth protecting if the deployment allows an FP32 stage.

4. Layer sensitivity

The Detect head (layer 24) costs 7.0%, far more than any other layer that was actually quantized. The next largest are layer 23, the last neck block feeding the head (1.0%), and layer 2 (0.9%). Caveat: in this test, layers 11 and 15 (the upsample layers, which hold no weights) received no quantization at all when isolated, because ONNX Runtime inserts no quantize/dequantize operations around them on their own. Their zero is therefore not a result, and they are drawn hatched in Figure 3. All other layers were confirmed to contain quantization operations. The study supports protecting the output end of the network; it does not show that any particular layer is free to quantize.

Speed. Quantization is working: ONNX Runtime executes the INT8 designs with integer convolution kernels. Each model was timed over 9 interleaved rounds (fresh process per round). On this CPU, INT8 ONNX models take 11 to 25% less time per image than FP32, the all-INT8 TFLite models 21 to 22% less, and the TFLite designs that keep an FP32 stage (M2, M3) 4 to 11% less, because that stage still runs as float. In every one of the 9 rounds each ONNX INT8 model was faster than the FP32 model, and each TFLite INT8 pipeline was faster in at least 8 of 9. Timing is noisy, though: the same model varied by up to 29% between rounds (laptop CPU frequency and temperature drift), so small gaps between INT8 variants (for example per-channel versus per-tensor) are not meaningful, while the gap to FP32 is. A per-operation profile of the ONNX models explains why the gain is not larger: INT8 convolutions are faster (8.0 ms against 12.6 ms for FP32), but the SiLU activation (a sigmoid followed by a multiply) costs 5.3 ms in the INT8 model against 0.5 ms in FP32, where it is fused into the convolution, so most of the convolution saving is spent on activations. Quantizing the neck does not speed it up: the neck region takes 11.1 ms in M3 and 10.5 ms in M1 against 5.3 ms in FP32, which is why M2 (neck left in FP32) is the fastest ONNX design. (Profiler kernel times include profiling overhead and do not add up to wall-clock time; this is how ONNX Runtime on this CPU behaves and says nothing about an NPU.) None of this is NPU speed. An NPU executes only INT8 operations, so on a real board the FP32 stages of M2 and M3 would fall back to the host CPU, and the ranking could differ substantially. That needs measuring on the target hardware.

Memory. Table 5 gives the memory a model adds to a process that already has the runtime loaded, at load time and at its peak during inference (which includes the activation tensors). The FP32 ONNX model adds 62 MB at peak; the all-INT8 ONNX models add 16 to 17% less. The ONNX models that keep FP32 parts (M2, M3) add 6 to 8% more than FP32: a graph that mixes INT8 and FP32 has to hold tensors in both forms around the boundaries (a likely explanation, not verified). In TFLite the effect is clearer: the FP32 pipeline adds 108 MB, the all-INT8 pipelines 35 to 36% less, and M2 and M3 10 to 12% less. Memory added at load is similar across ONNX models (about 16 to 17 MB), probably because the runtime re-packs weights whatever their precision (not verified). The runtime's own baseline (about 56 MB for ONNX Runtime and 261 MB for TensorFlow) is excluded. On a memory-limited board, activation memory matters more than the file size.

Why ONNX and TFLite differ for the same design

The FP32 baselines are identical (mAP50-95 0.2811). Quantization is not: ONNX Runtime calibrates with percentile clipping, while the TFLite converter only offers min-max. Percentile ignores rare outliers and so gives finer steps for the typical values. Rebuilding the ONNX models with min-max calibration removes most of the gap (Table 5): the remaining differences are in the range of -0.18 to +0.10 mAP points, while the percentile versions are +0.37 to +0.77 points above TFLite. The gap is a calibration effect, not a property of the format.

Why INT8 is not 2x or 4x faster here

  • The convolutions are only part of the time. In FP32 the SiLU activation is fused into the convolution. In INT8 it is a separate sigmoid and multiply on every layer, and its cost grows with the size of the activation tensor, which is largest in the first layers.
  • Other operators do not speed up: in ONNX Runtime the Resize and Concat steps of the neck need extra requantization work, so quantizing the neck (M3) is slower than leaving it in FP32 (M2). This was seen in the per-operation profile (results/profile.json).
  • Result with SiLU: INT8 reaches 1.33x at best in ONNX Runtime and 1.47x in OpenVINO (Tables 2 and 3), and no more in TFLite on this CPU.
  • Result with ReLU in the largest layers (Detect head bit-identical): the INT8 convolutions fuse their activation. With 12 of 57 activations as ReLU (R55) OpenVINO reaches 1.70x (head FP32) and 1.81x (all INT8) over its own FP32 baseline at 92 to 93% of the accuracy. With 29 of 57 (R80) it reaches 1.88x and 2.01x, at about 88 to 89% of the accuracy. The 2x mark is reached by the all-INT8 R80 model only at the cost of about 12% of the accuracy. A smaller input size does better: 2x at 95% or more of the accuracy (Section 6).
  • A 4x gain needs hardware where INT8 is not just faster but the only path (an NPU); this CPU cannot show that.

The R55 and R80 models (ReLU activations)

R55 changed 12 of the 57 activations (layers 0 to 4 and 17, about 55% of the activation work) from SiLU to ReLU; R80 changed 29 (81% of the activation work, layers 0 to 6, 13, 17 and 20) and the network was fine-tuned with distillation from the original model through a frozen Detect head. v1 used an abrupt swap and short training; v2 used a gradual swap, ground-truth loss, EMA and 24 epochs. The Detect head weights are identical to the original (verified by comparing every parameter).

  • FP32 accuracy: R55 v1 0.2514 (89.4% retained), R55 v2 0.2624 (93.4%), R80 0.2518 (89.6%). Replacing more activations buys speed and costs accuracy: each extra step is roughly 0.3x speed for 3 to 4 points.
  • Fastest model keeping 95% or more of the accuracy: 512 px M3, NNCF, 2.14x versus the OpenVINO FP32 baseline, at 95.7% retained.
  • For comparison, the best SiLU model that keeps 97% or more (OpenVINO M3, NNCF) is 1.41x over OpenVINO FP32. The trade is roughly 6 points of retained accuracy for about 0.4x more speed.

A smaller input size

Computation falls with the square of the input size, so the plain network (SiLU, no retraining, Detect head untouched) was exported at 512, 448 and 416 pixels, quantized with the same OpenVINO procedure (calibration images letterboxed to the same size) and scored on all 5000 images (Table 3). Accuracy stays high because most COCO objects are large enough, and speed rises more than with any activation change:

  • 640 px (reference): M3 1.41x at 97.7% retained.
  • 512 px: M3 2.14x at 95.7% (all-INT8 M1 2.23x at 95.0%): 2x at 95% or more of the accuracy.
  • 448 px: M3 2.73x at 92.2%.
  • 416 px: M3 3.14x at 88.9%.

Speed-ups are against the 640 px FP32 model, since that is the baseline the lower-resolution models replace. Small objects are the ones lost first, so this is the lever to choose only if the application's objects are not tiny.

Combinations that did not help. The ReLU models were also fine-tuned and exported at 512 px (R55 at 512: 2.58x at 88.8%), and the plain network was fine-tuned at 448 px with the head frozen (86.1% retained after INT8, against 92.2% without fine-tuning). Both are worse than simply lowering the resolution of the original network: with the head frozen, retraining on 9,500 images moved the backbone away from what the head expects more than it helped.

Limitations

  • All timings are from a laptop CPU; an NPU's ranking and speed-ups can differ. The exact target board is not known.
  • Round-to-round spread on this laptop is large (see the Round spread column), so differences of a few percent between INT8 variants are not meaningful; differences to FP32 are.
  • The R55 and R80 models are not available as TFLite; TFLite results cover the SiLU designs only.
  • OpenVINO INT8 models were quantized with NNCF (mixed preset, 500 calibration images); results for ONNX Runtime QDQ and OpenVINO are different quantizers and runtimes, not one model on two engines.

10. Recommendations

  • Use per-channel quantization wherever the target runtime supports it.
  • Normalise the box output to 0-1 before quantizing any model that includes the head.
  • For accuracy first, choose M2; for the best size-accuracy balance, choose M3; choose M1 only if the whole model must run as INT8 (for example entirely on an NPU), accepting the accuracy cost.
  • Do not spend effort on calibration set size beyond about 100 images for this model.
  • Next: time the candidates on the target board, and try a mixed design that keeps only the most sensitive layers (23 and 24) in FP32.

11. Limitations

  • No NPU measurements. Timing is from a desktop CPU (ONNX Runtime and TFLite with XNNPACK). It is a proxy only.
  • Different calibration methods in ONNX (percentile) and TFLite (min-max) mean the two formats are not like-for-like; the TFLite converter offers no percentile option.
  • Memory is the increase in resident memory of a desktop process (sampled every 2 ms), not NPU or device memory, and it excludes the runtime's own baseline.
  • Layer sensitivity uses the first 1000 images and one layer at a time; interactions between layers are not captured.
  • Data. COCO val2017 only. Results on other image domains may differ.

Appendix. Terms

mAP50-95 / mAP50
The standard detector accuracy score (0 to 1), averaged over overlap thresholds 0.50 to 0.95, or at 0.50 only.
Precision, recall, F1
Of reported detections, the share that are right; of real objects, the share found; and their harmonic mean.
Per-channel / per-tensor
One INT8 scale per output channel of each convolution, or one per layer.
Calibration
Running sample images through the model to choose the INT8 value ranges. Percentile ignores rare outliers; min-max uses the extremes.
Backbone, neck (PANet), head
The three stages of YOLOv5: feature extraction, feature fusion, and the Detect layer that outputs boxes, objects and classes.
Compression ratio
FP32 file size divided by the model's file size (same format).
